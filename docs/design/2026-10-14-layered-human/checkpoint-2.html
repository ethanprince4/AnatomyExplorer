A. Labels

Today’s labels are small dark chips over the body. That works for four names, but a lesson or a section can need eighteen, and then the chips start to look like software. Each option is shown on the atlas with twelve names and on a new section screen with eighteen. Every anchor comes from the app’s own picking data.

Today

1Free lettering recommended

Each name is lettered on the wall beside its structure; a name goes on a small chip over the body only when there is no clear wall close enough.

The agent’s pick: on the atlas every name sits right beside its structure, with no chip. I agree for the atlas. On the section, though, the six chips clustered over the heart are exactly the software look we wanted to lose (open the actual-size crop). One way out is to switch to the numbered key of option 3 when a view has more names than fit on the wall; the website already does this between desktop and phone.

2Caption rails

The names stack on two rails beside the specimen, in height order, each joined to its point by a leader with one bend.

3Numbered key

Small numbered dots sit on the body, and the names are a numbered list on the wall.

Notes

  • Caption rails draw leaders of about 500 px across the cut face on the section, and lean toward the Plate direction.
  • The numbered key costs an eye jump for every name. Hovering a dot or a list row would light the pair; that is described but not drawn.
  • Three muscles had no visible pixels at 26% deep, so their neighbours are labelled instead: Sternohyoid, Temporal bone and Internal oblique.

B. The depth gauge

The gauge is how you know how deep you are, and it is the one control every 3D room shares. In all three options the stops now sit at their true depths, “Superficial” and “Deep” mark the ends, “Show only this layer” sits next to the stop it acts on, and the knob works from the keyboard.

Today

The three side by side, at actual size

1Rule

A measuring rule hung beside the specimen, with fine marks every 2% and the eight stops named in italic at their real depths.

2Strata recommended

A core sample: eight stacked bands, each as tall as its layer, with the current band lit; clicking a band means show only this layer.

The agent’s pick: it makes “layered” literal, and the layer you are in is something you can point at. I would pick 1, Rule, instead. The website’s first screen already uses the rule, so the app and the site would match. Strata’s lit band is faint on its own (it reads by its edges), and it needs a small change to how “Show only this layer” works so that the lit band is exactly what stays.

3Title

The page title carries the depth, such as “Superficial muscles, 26% deep”, and the gauge shrinks to a slim rule whose names appear only while you drag it or focus it.

Notes

  • Option 3 drops the descriptive title “Muscles, anterior view”, and its checkbox ends up far from the gauge.
  • In all three, the up arrow goes shallower, to match the picture. That is the opposite of most sliders.

C. The libraries

Lessons and Histology are where students pick what to study next. Models already stands its specimens lit on the wall; the question is how much of that to give the other two. Each option is shown on Lessons and on a new Histology library screen.

Today

1Catalogue

Typed lists, like an index card drawer: a number, a title, progress and a count, with a small slide picture beside each tissue.

2Vitrines

3Lit row recommended

Three lit vitrines come first, for what you do next (Continue, Review and the next lab, or the last tissue and its two neighbours), and the catalogue of option 1 follows.

The agent’s pick, and mine: the museum moment goes where the eye lands first, on what you do next, and the fast list stays where you scan. Vitrines is the most beautiful, but it shows 14 of 101 tissues at a time and needs about 26 new lab figures.

New rooms and states

These are not choices. Look them over and flag anything that seems off.

The model viewer

Click the kidney in Models and it opens into its own room: the parts list on the left, the model under the light, its thirteen stored views as a strip beneath, and the Reveal controls on the right, laid out like the gauge.

Search, as a catalogue drawer

Ctrl K drops a dark drawer from the header, with results grouped by kind, Latin names in italic and the system on the line below; the results are what the app’s own search returns for “biceps”.

A lesson that points at the model

Terms with a label get a brass hairline; hover the term or its label and the pair lights up together, and hovering a step dot names that step.

Practice feedback in the model

A wrong click gets a plain ring and the name of what you clicked; a right answer gets a brass ring and “Yes”; twelve ticks along the foot show the session.

What is not finished

So that nothing here looks more finished than it is:

  • The model viewer’s label points were placed by eye; the kidney has no picking data yet. Seven of its thirteen view tiles are empty, because those views have no render.
  • Two Histology slides are still grey placeholders (keratinised stratified squamous, and heart valves). A few micrographs are soft, and one shows a single panel of a larger figure with a trick the Qt app cannot use.
  • In Vitrines, three lab figures stand in for the right ones, and in Lit row the Review figure stands in for what you actually practised.
  • The practice and lesson marks are rings, not a tint on the structure, because these plates have no per-structure images. The search result after Enter is not drawn.
  • “Show only this layer” switched on is shown in gauge close-ups only, and the counts do not change as you drag.
  • On a phone, the body on the website’s first screen sits on a slightly lighter rectangle of wall: the plates carry their own light, and its edges show at that width.
  • On the website, the selection at 16% is put together from the 8% picture, and “peeled away” at 40% was judged by eye.
